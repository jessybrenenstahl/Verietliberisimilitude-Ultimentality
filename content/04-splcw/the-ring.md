# The Ring

**The Ring is the recurring organization of the five SPLCW functions, open to the consequences of their action.** Warden, Captive, Logician, Poet, and Sculptor form a connected operation whose return passes through a changed world.

## The figure and the operation

The [[epigraph|epigraph]] supplies a figure of interdependent elements and an act of pinning. The Ring reads those elements together rather than treating them as isolated symbols. Its operational counterpart is:

```text
Warden → Captive → Logician → Poet → Sculptor → changed world → Warden
```

The figure gives the reader a way to grasp recurrence. The [[operator-chain|operator chain]] specifies the functions and their return. Neither the words of the image nor their grammatical form determine the duration of a real act.

## Connected and open

The loop is connected because its effects can return as new significance. It is open because that return is not wholly authored by the participant and can alter subsequent operation. An action can finish while its consequences remain answerable; a participant can cease while a trace continues elsewhere.

Connectivity, causal openness, and epistemic finality are distinct. Describing a circuit as closed is not itself self-certification. The failure arises when the participant treats its own account as sufficient to settle every returned consequence or challenge. The [[witness-outside-the-ring|witness]] names the corrective independence that prevents that closure of judgment.

## Five functions

The present Ring articulates five functions. Its maximal-incomplete image means that the depicted complement is filled while participation remains open; it does not establish that no other functional carving can preserve or improve the operation. An alternative must recover the distinctions and dependencies the current arrangement supplies.

The witness is a relation of answerability to the operation, rather than an additional role inserted merely to make the count larger. Functions, their carriers, and the positions from which they are examined must be kept distinct.
